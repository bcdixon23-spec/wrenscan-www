Is your router on the FBI's list? The 20 models criminals hijack most

26 November 2026 · 4 min read

Written 26 November 2026. We make Wrenscan, which is mentioned at the end.

In March 2026 the FBI and police in several European countries shut down a service called SocksEscort. For about six years it had been breaking into home routers, quietly installing malware called AVrecon, and renting those routers out so that criminals could hide behind ordinary people's internet connections. Anything they did online then looked as though it came from the router owner's home.

The FBI's notice says about 369,000 devices in roughly 163 countries were taken over and sold this way since 2020. Most were home and small-office routers with known flaws that had never been patched.

The 20 models the FBI saw most often

The malware targets about 1,200 models from Cisco, D-Link, Hikvision, MikroTik, Netgear, TP-Link and Zyxel. These are the 20 the FBI found most often among the infected devices:

Being on the list does not mean your router is infected. It means your model is one that criminals have been getting into, usually through a flaw that was never patched on that device.

How to find your router's model

  1. Look at the label on the bottom or back of the router. The model is printed there, often next to "Model" or "M/N".
  2. If your internet provider supplied the box, the model is usually on that label too. Several of the Zyxel models above were handed out by providers.
  3. Or open the router's admin page in a browser (often 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1). The model is normally shown on the first screen.

If your model is on the list

The FBI's advice, in plain terms:

  1. Check whether it still gets updates. Search the maker's website for your model and "end of life". If the maker has stopped supporting it, the FBI says to consider replacing it with one that still gets security updates. No setting fixes a router that will never be patched again.
  2. If it is still supported, install the latest firmware from the router's admin page, and switch on automatic updates if the router offers them. Many routers never update unless you tell them to.
  3. Change the admin password if it is still the one printed on the label or a default such as "admin".
  4. Turn off remote administration (sometimes called remote management or WAN access), so the admin page can't be reached from the internet.
  5. Restart it now and then. The FBI says a restart can interrupt some infections, but it does not stop the router being infected again. A factory reset followed by the latest firmware sometimes helps too, though the notice warns that some versions of the malware block this.

If you run a small office, the notice adds one more point: keep routers and cameras apart from the computers that hold your important data where you can, because a hijacked router can be a way further in.

If your model isn't on the list

Good, but the list is only the top 20 of about 1,200 models. The same habits apply to every router: keep the firmware current, replace it when the maker stops supporting it, change the default password, and keep the admin page off the internet. Checking without turning the router over

Wrenscan is a free Windows app that finds every device on your network and tells you, in plain English, if your router or camera is one of the 20 models on the FBI's list. It also tells you if the model has a flaw on the US cyber agency CISA's list of flaws used in real attacks. It works from what the device says about itself, so it stays quiet when a device doesn't announce its model, and it cannot see inside a router to say whether that router has been infected.
