Is my router secure? A 10-minute checklist

25 November 2026 · 3 min read

Your router is the front door of your home network: everything you do online passes through it, and every device behind it relies on it. It's also the device people set up once and never look at again. Here's a ten-minute check, most important first. You'll need your router's admin page: type your router's address (usually 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1; on Windows, ipconfig shows it as the Default Gateway) into a browser and sign in.

1. Change the admin password

If you still sign in with the password printed on the sticker, or worse, admin/password, change it to a long, unique one and store it in a password manager. This is the password that controls everything else.

2. Update the firmware

Router makers fix security holes through firmware updates, and attackers actively exploit the ones that aren't patched. Look for Firmware update, Router update or Administration, and turn on automatic updates if your router offers them. If your router no longer gets updates at all, it's time to replace it.

3. Use WPA2 or WPA3 for Wi-Fi

In the wireless settings, the security mode should be WPA3 or WPA2-PSK (AES), or a mixed WPA2/WPA3 mode. Avoid WEP, WPA (TKIP) or "open". Use a Wi-Fi passphrase of at least 12 characters.

4. Turn off WPS

Wi-Fi Protected Setup lets devices join with a button press or a PIN. The PIN method in particular has a long history of weaknesses. Unless you rely on it, turn it off.

5. Turn off remote management

Remote management (or "remote access", "web access from WAN") lets the router's admin page be reached from the internet. Unless you know you need it, it should be off.

6. Think about UPnP

UPnP lets devices and games open ports in your router automatically. It's convenient, but it also means any device on your network (including a compromised one) can expose itself to the internet without asking. If you don't play online games or run anything that needs it, consider turning it off; if you leave it on, check which ports are open from time to time.

7. Review port forwarding

Look at Port forwarding or Virtual servers. Each rule exposes a device to the whole internet. Delete any you don't recognise or no longer need, especially for remote desktop (port 3389), file sharing (445) or a camera's web page.

8. Put guests and gadgets on a guest network

Most routers can run a separate guest network. Use it for visitors, and ideally for smart-home gadgets too, so a cheap smart plug can't reach your laptop.
